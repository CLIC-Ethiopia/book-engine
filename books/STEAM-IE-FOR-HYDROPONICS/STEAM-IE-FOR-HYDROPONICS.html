No Need for Soil

Why dirt is just an anchor, and nutrients in water do the real work

Plant two kale seedlings: one in dry dirt, and one in clean water mixed with plant food.

Soil is not food. Dirt is mostly rocks and clay holding the plant upright. A root in dirt spends energy pushing through hard ground to find food.

Plants cannot eat solid dirt. They only drink nutrients dissolved in water.

Dirt is only an anchor. Water soluble nutrients are the meal.

DO THIS TODAY

Take a handful of garden soil, drop it into a glass of water, and stir it. Watch how dirt settles to the bottom while clear water holds dissolved minerals.

How Plants Drink

Capillary action, and why roots pull water without soil

Put a white flower in a glass of water with food colouring. Come back in two hours. The petals are not white any more.

That climb against gravity has a name: capillary action. Water sticks to thin tubes, and the thinner the tube, the higher it climbs. Roots are made of those thin tubes, so in a hydroponic system the plant pulls the same way it always has.

The plant was always going to drink. You just gave it a faster way to reach the cup.

TRY THIS WEEKEND

Set a white flower in a cup of water with food colouring. Look again in two hours.

Water Chemistry

pH and dissolved oxygen, and why both matter to a root

Pour a glass of plain water. Squeeze half a lemon into a second glass. Taste both. The second is sour. That is pH. Most plants want a pH between 5.5 and 6.5. Too far either way and the roots stop taking in nutrients.

The other number is dissolved oxygen. Roots breathe. A small air stone or a splashing pump puts air back into the solution.

Two numbers decide if the plant eats well: how sour the water is, and how much air is in it.

DO THIS TODAY

Take one glass of plain tap water, one glass with a spoon of lemon juice. Taste both. That difference is pH.

Where the water goes when the pump forgets to stop
