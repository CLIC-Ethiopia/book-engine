# STEAM-IE · No. 01 Science

## Moisture Equilibrium &amp; Psychrometric Drying Curves

#### Water Leaves Fruit Only When Vapor Pressure Drops Below the Internal Tissue Threshold

![AI Image Prompt: Technical vector visual layout showing a dual-axis psychrometric drying curve chart. Left vertical axis shows Fruit Moisture Content (%), right vertical axis shows Drying Rate (g/hr), and horizontal axis shows Drying Time (Hours). Clear labeled phases: Constant Rate Period (surface evaporation) and Falling Rate Period (internal moisture diffusion). Below the graph, a micro-cross-section diagram shows a fruit slice with internal moisture moving toward the surface via capillary action under heat. Clean technical vector blueprint style with sharp lines and labels in Space Grotesk font.]

Fruit does not dry simply because hot air blows over it[cite: 1]. Drying occurs due to a vapor pressure differential between the moist moisture layer inside the fruit tissue and the surrounding hot, dry air[cite: 1]. When hot air enters the drying chamber, it lowers relative humidity ($RH$), expanding the air's capacity to absorb evaporated water vapor from the crop.

The drying process follows two distinct thermodynamic phases: the **Constant Rate Period** and the **Falling Rate Period**. In the initial constant rate phase, surface moisture evaporates freely as if from an open water surface. The rate is limited solely by external heat transfer and surrounding air velocity.

Once surface moisture is depleted, the fruit enters the critical falling rate period. Drying speed is now governed by internal moisture diffusion through cell walls to the surface. Blowing air faster at this stage wastes power; elevation of thermal energy and strict control of vapor pressure are required to draw bound water out without damaging fruit quality.

[ACTION CALLOUT: MEASURE IT] Cut three identical 5mm mango slices. Weigh them, place them in a 50°C drying chamber, and re-weigh them every 30 minutes for 4 hours to plot your first local moisture loss rate curve.

# STEAM-IE · No. 02 Science

## Solar Irradiance, Collector Absorption &amp; Thermal Energy Transfer

#### Dark Surfaces Convert Solar Photons into Heat while Transparent Glazing Traps the Infrared Wave

![AI Image Prompt: Clean technical schematic showing a solar thermal collector cross-section. Rays of sunlight (UV/Visible spectrum) pass through a top clear glass/polycarbonate sheet, hitting a matte-black corrugated metal absorber plate. The dark plate converts photons into infrared radiation (heat), which is trapped under the glazing (Greenhouse Effect). Cool air enters from the bottom, absorbs thermal energy from the hot plate, and exits as high-temperature dry air at the top. Labeled vector schematic, blueprint aesthetic.]

Sunlight consists of shortwave radiation that passes through transparent glazing like glass or UV-treated polycarbonate. When these photons hit a dark, non-reflective absorber surface—such as matte-black painted corrugated sheet metal—they are absorbed and re-emitted as longwave infrared thermal energy (heat).

Standard glass and polycarbonate sheets allow shortwave solar radiation to enter easily, but block longwave infrared radiation from escaping. This fundamental thermodynamic trap, known as the greenhouse effect, locks heat inside the collector box, rapidly heating the passing air column.

Thermal energy transfers from the hot absorber plate to the air stream through thermal convection. Maximizing the surface contact area between the air stream and the metal plate—using corrugated ridges or baffle plates—dramatically increases heat transfer efficiency before air reaches the drying chamber.

[ACTION CALLOUT: TRY THIS WEEKEND] Paint one sheet of scrap metal matte black and leave another bare shiny steel. Place both under direct midday sunlight in Addis Ababa for 15 minutes, then measure their surface temperatures with an infrared thermometer to quantify the thermal gain.

# STEAM-IE · No. 03 Science

## Natural Convection vs. Forced Air Chimney Dynamics

#### Heated Air Expands and Rises, Creating Buoyancy-Driven Drafts Without Electric Power

![AI Image Prompt: Vector diagram illustrating thermal buoyancy draft in a solar dryer. A tall vertical exhaust chimney is shown attached to a drying cabinet. Hot air (red arrows) becomes less dense, creating a pressure vacuum ($\Delta P$) that pulls fresh cool ambient air (blue arrows) through the bottom intake vents. Side-by-side comparison shows: (A) Low chimney height causing sluggish airflow, vs. (B) High chimney height creating a strong continuous buoyancy draft. Blueprint technical style.]

Air expands as it absorbs thermal energy, decreasing in density compared to the cooler ambient air outside the dryer[cite: 1]. This density differential creates natural thermal buoyancy—hot air naturally rises toward the highest point of the system, driving continuous airflow across drying trays[cite: 1].

A tall, dark-painted exhaust chimney amplifies this stack effect. As air inside the tall chimney heats up further from solar exposure, it rushes upward, creating a low-pressure vacuum at the base of the drying cabinet that continuously pulls fresh, ambient air through lower intake vents[cite: 1].

While natural convection requires zero electricity, its flow rate fluctuates with solar intensity[cite: 1]. Forced air systems use low-power DC fans to maintain a fixed air velocity across trays, preventing stagnant moisture buildup during cloudy periods or high humidity mornings.

[ACTION CALLOUT: BUILD THIS ONCE] Construct a simple 1-meter tall black PVC chimney pipe over a closed wooden box with a bottom intake hole. Hold a lighted incense stick near the intake vent under direct sun to visually verify the thermal draft velocity.

## UV Spectrum Degradation &amp; Color/Nutrient Preservation

#### Direct Sunlight Destroys Essential Vitamins and Bleaches Export-Grade Fruit Visuals

![AI Image Prompt: Comparative technical diagram showing solar fruit drying methods. Left panel: Direct Sun Exposure showing UV rays hitting sliced mangoes directly, causing pigment degradation (browning/bleaching) and Vitamin C destruction. Right panel: Indirect Solar Drying where a solar thermal collector heats air separately, sending only hot air into a shaded, UV-blocked drying cabinet, preserving bright orange fruit color and nutrient integrity. High-contrast vector design.]

Direct exposure to solar ultraviolet (UV) radiation breaks down organic compounds, degrading critical nutrients such as Vitamin C, Vitamin A (beta-carotene), and heat-sensitive antioxidants in drying fruits. UV light also bleaches natural fruit pigments, turning vibrant mangoes and pineapples into dull, unappealing brown slices.

Indirect solar drying isolates the solar collection process from the food processing chamber[cite: 1]. Sunlight heats the air inside a separate thermal collector unit, and only the heated air enters the opaque, shaded drying chamber to dry the fruit.

By completely blocking direct UV radiation from reaching the crop surface, indirect drying preserves visual color intensity, retains nutrient profiles, and meets strict export-grade quality standards demanded by premium retail buyers[cite: 1].

[ACTION CALLOUT: DO THIS TODAY] Place two identical slices of papaya under the sun for 6 hours: cover one with a dark cardboard shadow shield allowing air circulation, and leave the other completely exposed to direct sunlight. Compare visual color retention side by side.

# STEAM-IE · No. 05 Science

## Water Activity ($a_w$) vs. Mold/Bacterial Spoilage Thresholds

#### Total Moisture Content Lies; Available Free Water Determines Absolute Shelf Stability

![AI Image Prompt: Detailed microbiological threshold chart displaying Water Activity ($a_w$) levels from 1.0 down to 0.2. Annotations show critical growth limits: Bacteria ($a_w < 0.91$), Yeasts ($a_w < 0.88$), Mold ($a_w < 0.80$), Osmophilic Yeasts ($a_w < 0.60$). Target dried fruit shelf-stable zone highlighted between $0.55$ and $0.62 a_w$. Vector chart with clean typography and biological icons.]

Moisture content measures the total weight percentage of water in fruit, but **Water Activity (**$a_w$**)** measures the amount of unbound, "free" water available for microbial growth and chemical reactions. Two fruits with the exact same moisture percentage can have drastically different shelf stability based on their $a_w$ values.

Microorganisms require specific free water thresholds to reproduce: pathogenic bacteria stop multiplying below $0.91 a_w$, most yeasts cease activity below $0.88 a_w$, and common storage molds are inhibited below $0.80 a_w$. To guarantee a 12-month shelf-life without chemical preservatives, target an $a_w$ between $0.55$ and $0.62$.

Achieving this critical $a_w$ threshold prevents toxic mold growth (such as aflatoxins) and stops enzymatic browning reactions during storage, ensuring packaged dried fruits remain safe, pliable, and shelf-stable in ambient tropical storage conditions.

[ACTION CALLOUT: READ IT TODAY] Check a digital water activity meter or salt calibration table. Mix 35g of pure table salt in 100ml of water inside a sealed container; verify that the ambient $RH$ inside the sealed head-space stabilizes at exactly $75\% RH$ ($0.75 a_w$) at 25°C.

# STEAM-IE · No. 06 Science

## Sugar Concentration (Brix) Dynamics &amp; Case Hardening Prevention

#### Drying Too Fast Traps Internal Moisture Behind an Impermeable Hard Outer Crust

![AI Image Prompt: Vector cross-section comparison of two drying fruit slices under high temperature vs controlled temperature. Left image (Case Hardening): High initial heat (&gt;65°C) flashes surface moisture away, forming a rubbery, hardened outer sugar skin while the center remains wet and raw. Right image (Optimal Drying): Moderate initial heat (50-55°C) allows uniform moisture migration from center to outer skin, producing a soft, pliable, fully dry slice. Blueprint technical style.]

As water evaporates from fruit slices, natural sugars (sucrose, fructose, glucose) concentrate near the outer surface layer[cite: 1]. If drying temperatures are pushed too high early in the processing cycle, surface moisture evaporates faster than internal moisture can migrate outward.

This rapid surface desiccation causes proteins and concentrated sugars to shrink and seal, creating a tough, rubbery exterior skin—a defect known as **case hardening**. This hardened outer crust traps residual water inside the core, leading to internal rotting and mold once packaged.

To prevent case hardening in high-sugar East African fruits like ripe mangoes and pineapples, initial drying temperatures must be kept below 55°C with moderate air circulation. This allows internal moisture migration to keep pace with surface evaporation until the critical moisture mass is removed.

[ACTION CALLOUT: MEASURE IT] Take a refractometer and record the initial °Brix (sugar level) of fresh mango juice. If the fruit reads above 18°Brix, lower initial dryer air intake temperatures by 5°C to avoid premature surface crusting.

## East African Microclimates: Ambient RH vs. Highland Thermal Lift

#### Adapting Dryer Operation to High-Altitude Dry Air vs. Moist Coastal Basins

![AI Image Prompt: Vector map and climate graph of East Africa showing distinct drying operational zones: Addis Ababa Highland (High Altitude, Low Ambient Air Pressure, Cool/Dry) vs Hawassa Rift Valley (Moderate Altitude, Humid) vs Coastal Mombasa (Sea Level, High Moisture). Annotated callout boxes show required airflow adjustments and solar thermal collector sizing modifications for each geographic elevation. Clean technical layout.]

East African geography presents radically different drying environments. High-altitude regions like Addis Ababa (2,350m elevation) benefit from lower atmospheric pressure and low ambient relative humidity, which naturally accelerates evaporation rates even at moderate temperatures[cite: 1].

Conversely, lower-elevation rift valley zones (e.g., Hawassa, Adama) or coastal hubs experience elevated relative humidity and higher ambient air densities[cite: 1]. In these humid environments, incoming air holds less additional moisture capacity, requiring larger solar thermal collector surfaces and boosted forced-air flow rates to achieve identical drying timelines.

Understanding your local elevation's psychrometric profile prevents costly equipment mis-sizing. Highland operators can run smaller collector plates but require night-time thermal insulation, while lowland operators need higher volume air changes to strip water vapor out of humid ambient intake air.

[ACTION CALLOUT: DO THIS TODAY] Look up your local city's elevation and average ambient relative humidity ($RH$) for the current month. Calculate your atmospheric pressure correction factor using standard barometric tables to fine-tune drying times.

## Low-Power 12V DC Axial Fans &amp; Airflow Rate Calibration

#### Sizing Brushless Cooling Fans for Continuous Moisture Removal on Solar-Direct Circuits

![AI Image Prompt: Technical line-art schematic of a 12V DC brushless axial fan setup in a solar dryer air intake duct. Labeled elements: Anemometer measuring air velocity ($m/s$), PWM Speed Controller dial, Inline Fuse (3A), and 12V DC Solar/Battery leads. A side equation block shows the conversion of air velocity ($m/s$) multiplied by duct cross-sectional area ($m^2$) to calculate volumetric flow rate ($m^3/h$ or CFM).]

Passive natural airflow can stall during overcast mornings or humid afternoons, leading to fruit spoilage. Low-power 12V DC brushless axial computer-style fans provide cheap, highly controllable forced ventilation powered directly by small solar PV panels or battery banks[cite: 1].

Airflow velocity must match total wet fruit capacity[cite: 1]. Excessive air speed cools the solar collector before thermal energy transfers to the air column; insufficient air speed allows moisture-laden air to saturate the drying chamber, causing condensation on upper trays.

Calibrate airflow using an inline Pulse Width Modulation (PWM) speed controller. Measure face velocity using a digital vane anemometer at the exhaust port, targeting a uniform airspeed between $0.5\text{ m/s}$ and $1.5\text{ m/s}$ across all active tray gaps[cite: 1].

[ACTION CALLOUT: MEASURE IT] Hold a pocket anemometer directly in front of your dryer fan exhaust duct. Adjust the PWM dial until the air velocity reads exactly $1.0\text{ m/s}$, and record the DC current draw on your multimeter.

# STEAM-IE · No. 10 Technology

## Thermal Energy Storage: Phase Change Materials &amp; Rock Beds

#### Storing Daytime Excess Solar Heat to Keep Air Moving and Drying After Sunset

![AI Image Prompt: Vector cross-sectional schematic of a solar thermal collector featuring an integrated Rock Bed / PCM thermal storage floor beneath the absorber plate. Diagram shows daytime solar charging (sunlight heating dark river rocks under the collector) and night-time heat release (stored thermal energy radiating warm air up through drying trays after dark). Heat flow arrows in red and blue, vector technical manual style.]

Solar dryers stop receiving thermal energy the moment the sun sets, yet semi-dried fruit sitting in a warm, dark cabinet will reabsorb ambient humidity and rot overnight[cite: 1]. Thermal Energy Storage (TES) systems store excess daytime heat to extend drying cycles past twilight.

Low-cost thermal storage beds utilize washed river gravel or basalt rocks packed inside the collector floor[cite: 1]. River rocks absorb surplus thermal energy during peak hours (11:00 AM – 2:00 PM) and gradually release heat back into the rising air stream well into the evening hours[cite: 1].

For higher thermal density, Phase Change Materials (PCM)—such as paraffin wax contained inside sealed copper tubes—absorb latent heat as they melt at 50°C during peak sun and release that latent heat as they solidify after dark, stabilizing night cabinet temperatures.

[ACTION CALLOUT: BUILD THIS ONCE] Fill the bottom 10cm of a solar thermal collector box with smooth black river stones (30-50mm diameter). Record cabinet air temperature at 8:00 PM compared to an empty collector box.

# STEAM-IE · No. 11 Technology

## Off-Grid Solar PV, Charge Controller &amp; Battery Sizing

#### Calculating 24/7 Electrical Autonomy for Forced Air Fans and Night Control Systems

![AI Image Prompt: Wiring schematic for an off-grid solar power system powering dryer accessories. Components shown: 50W Monocrystalline PV Panel -&gt; 10A PWM Solar Charge Controller -&gt; 12V 20Ah Deep-Cycle AGM Battery -&gt; 12V DC Axial Fans &amp; Microcontroller Logger. Detailed text callouts show system wattages, daily amp-hour calculations, and safety fuse locations.]

Forced-air fans and electronic loggers require reliable off-grid power to prevent drying halts during frequent grid power cuts[cite: 1]. An autonomous 12V DC power package requires balancing PV panel generation against battery amp-hour capacity.

Calculate daily electrical load in Amp-Hours ($\text{Ah}$). Two 12V fans drawing 0.3A each running 24 hours consumes:

$$
\text{Load} = 2 \times 0.3\text{A} \times 24\text{h} = 14.4\text{Ah/day}
$$

To ensure continuous operation over two rainy days (autonomy factor of 2) without discharging a lead-acid battery beyond 50% Depth of Discharge ($\text{DoD}$), target a minimum battery capacity of:

$$
\text{Battery Size} = \frac{14.4\text{Ah} \times 2}{0.50} = 57.6\text{Ah}
$$

Match this with a 50W–80W solar PV panel and a 10A PWM charge controller to recharge the bank fully during peak daytime sun hours.

[ACTION CALLOUT: DO THE MATH] Sum the total current draw (Amps) of all 12V devices on your dryer. Multiply by 24 hours, then divide by 0.5 to determine the minimum Ah rating needed for your battery bank.

## IoT Airflow Sensors &amp; Differential Pressure Gauge Setup

#### Detecting Clogged Trays and Channel Air Bottlenecks Before Quality Suffers

![AI Image Prompt: Vector diagram showing differential pressure measurement across drying cabinet trays. When operators overpack drying trays with overlapping fruit slices, air channels clog, causing static backpressure that starves upper trays of airflow[cite: 1]. Differential pressure monitoring detects these hidden flow bottlenecks instantly[cite: 1].

## Solar Thermal Collector Design: Latitude-Matched Glazing Angle

#### Maximizing Year-Round Thermal Absorption by Matching Collector Tilt to Geographical Coordinates

![AI Image Prompt: Engineering drawing showing a solar thermal collector box tilt angle relative to incoming sun rays. Technical details: Ground horizontal baseline, collector elevation angle set to Latitude + 10 degrees (e.g., 19 degrees for Addis Ababa at ~9°N). Sun ray angles shown for Summer and Winter solstices hitting the collector surface perpendicularly for maximum energy capture. Clean vector blueprint.]

Solar thermal collectors achieve peak energy efficiency when sunlight strikes the glass cover at a perpendicular 90-degree angle. Because the sun's path shifts seasonally, collector tilt angles must be matched to local geographical latitude[cite: 1].

For year-round processing in East Africa (located near the equator, e.g., Addis Ababa at ~9°N latitude), set the fixed collector tilt angle to:

$$
\text{Tilt Angle} = \text{Local Latitude} + 10^\circ
$$

This additional 10-degree tilt ensures optimal solar photon capture during low-sun winter months and promotes rain shedding off clear glazing sheets[cite: 1].

Collectors must face directly true South if located in the Northern Hemisphere, or true North if located in the Southern Hemisphere. Avoid magnetic declination errors by aligning frames using geographical solar midday shadows rather than basic magnetic pocket compasses.

[ACTION CALLOUT: MEASURE IT] Check your city's latitude. Use an incline angle finder app on your smartphone held against your collector frame to set its angle precisely to Local Latitude $+ 10^\circ$.

This architectural separation allows precise environmental control: operators can adjust collector angles independently, add night-time thermal energy storage, and inspect drying trays in a shaded work space without dropping collector temperatures[cite: 1].

## Chimney Sizing &amp; Exhaust Damper Airflow Control

## Calculating Dryer Sizing: Fruit Wet Weight vs. Target Dry Yield

 sizing a solar dryer requires calculating moisture reduction ratios[cite: 1]. Fresh mangoes consist of approximately $82\%$ water and $18\%$ dry solids. Dried fruit targets $15\%$ residual moisture.

## Thermal Collector Efficiency &amp; Energy Balance Equations

#### Quantifying How Much Solar Energy Reaches the Air Stream versus Loss Rates

![AI Image Prompt: Mathematical balance sheet schematic of a solar thermal collector. Input vector arrow: Incoming Solar Radiation ($I = 900 \text{ W/m}^2 \times \text{Area}$). Output vectors: Useful Heat Absorbed by Air Stream ($Q_u$, green arrow) vs Thermal Losses ($Q_l$, top radiation, bottom conduction, side convection in red arrows). Collector Efficiency equation ($\eta_c$) highlighted in center box.]

Solar thermal collector efficiency ($\eta_c$) measures the percentage of raw solar radiation striking the glass cover that converts into useful heat in the air stream[cite: 1].

Calculate thermal energy gain ($Q_u$) in Watts using air mass flow rate ($\dot{m}$ in $\text{kg/s}$), specific heat capacity of air ($C_p \approx 1005 \text{ J/kg}\cdot^\circ\text{C}$), and temperature rise ($\Delta T = T_{\text{exit}} - T_{\text{ambient}}$):

$$
Q_u = \dot{m} \times C_p \times (T_{\text{exit}} - T_{\text{ambient}})
$$

Collector efficiency ($\eta_c$) is then calculated against total incoming solar irradiance ($I$ in $\text{W/m}^2$) over collector glass area ($A_c$ in $\text{m}^2$):

$$
\eta_c = \frac{Q_u}{A_c \times I} \times 100\%
$$

Well-designed solar thermal collectors achieve $40\%\text{ to }60\%$ thermal efficiency under midday sun[cite: 1].
