# STEAM-IE · No. 01 Science
## Soil Mechanics Composition
#### Test local soil fractions to build structural blocks without structural cracks.

![AI Image Prompt: Clean technical vector diagram showing a glass jar soil sediment test. Three distinct horizontal layers labeled Sand and Gravel at bottom, Silt in middle, Clay on top, with clear water above. Simple, high-contrast blueprint style.]

Soil is not dirt; it is a blend of aggregate, binder, and fine filler. To make durable interlocking blocks, you need 50–70% sand, 10–20% silt, and 10–20% clay. 

Sand provides structural strength, silt acts as a passive filler, and clay acts as a natural binder. Too much clay causes swelling and severe cracking during drying. Too little clay leaves blocks weak and crumbly.

Run a simple field jar test. Fill a straight-sided jar one-third full with soil, add water, shake vigorously for 2 minutes, and let it settle for 24 hours.

[ACTION CALLOUT: DO THIS TODAY] Collect three distinct soil samples from your site at 50 cm depth and run a 24-hour jar settlement test.

# STEAM-IE · No. 02 Science
## Clay Mineralogy Reactivity
#### Identify clay types to avoid catastrophic block swelling and collapse.

![AI Image Prompt: Vector diagram comparing Kaolinite stable parallel sheets versus Smectite expanding lattice structure with water molecules penetrating layers. Clear educational style.]

Not all clay behaves the same way when mixed with water and cement. Kaolinite clay is stable and ideal for earth block production.

Smectite and montmorillonite clays expand significantly when wet and shrink drastically when dry. Blocks made with expansive clays will crack and crumble within weeks.

Test for expansive clay using the Alizarin Red or simple drop-test method. If a wet soil ball sticks heavily to your palm and cracks severely when air-drying, it contains active expansive clays.

[ACTION CALLOUT: TRY THIS WEEKEND] Mold three 5 cm soil balls, let them dry in direct sunlight, and observe if severe surface fissures develop.

# STEAM-IE · No. 03 Science
## Cement Lime Hydration
#### Trigger chemical bonds to transform raw earth into artificial stone.

![AI Image Prompt: Technical micro-diagram showing cement hydration crystals forming interconnected bridges between sand particles. Minimal labels, vector line art.]

Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC) reacts with soil moisture to form calcium silicate hydrate (CSH) gels that bind sand particles together.

Adding 5–8% cement by weight turns water-vulnerable soil into a water-resistant, high-strength building block. Cement binds best with sandy soils.

If your soil has higher clay content (above 25%), use hydrated lime instead. Lime breaks down active clay molecules over 48 hours before forming permanent bonds.

[ACTION CALLOUT: MEASURE IT] Weigh 10 kg of dry soil and test a 6% cement mix (600 g) versus an 8% mix (800 g) to compare initial set times.

# STEAM-IE · No. 04 Science
## Curing Kinetics Moisture
#### Maintain continuous moisture to achieve maximum compressive block strength.

![AI Image Prompt: Vector illustration of stacked blocks under a clear plastic curing sheet with water droplets condensing inside. High-contrast technical layout.]

Hydration does not happen instantly; cement requires water to continuously form strength-giving crystals over 28 days.

If a freshly pressed block dries out in the sun, hydration stops permanently. The block will lose up to 60% of its potential strength.

Keep pressed blocks under plastic sheeting in the shade for the first 7 days, spraying them lightly with water twice daily.

[ACTION CALLOUT: BUILD THIS ONCE] Construct a shaded, wind-protected curing bay using local timber posts and 200-micron plastic sheeting.

# STEAM-IE · No. 05 Science
## Compaction Shear Strength
#### Apply high mechanical pressure to eliminate internal air voids.

![AI Image Prompt: Schematic showing soil particles before compression with large air voids versus after high compaction with locked particles. Clean blueprint style.]

Strength comes from density. Soil particles must be forced closely together to maximize friction and mechanical interlocking.

Compaction forces out trapped air, increasing density from 1,500 kg/m³ in loose soil to over 2,000 kg/m³ in pressed blocks.

Higher density reduces water absorption and dramatically increases resistance to shear stresses caused by wall loads.

[ACTION CALLOUT: MEASURE IT] Weigh a dry loose soil batch versus the final compressed block to calculate your machine's density compression ratio.

# STEAM-IE · No. 06 Science
## Particle Size Distribution
#### Balance coarse and fine particles for dense internal particle packing.

![AI Image Prompt: Graph showing an optimal grain size distribution curve with particle sizes from gravel to clay. Vector schematic with clean axes.]

Ideal soil for ICSEB contains a balanced mix of particle sizes from coarse sand (2 mm) down to fine clay (below 0.002 mm).

When smaller particles fill the spaces between larger ones, the total void space drops significantly. Less void space means less cement is required.

If your soil is too sandy, add fine clay soil. If your soil is too clay-heavy, blend in sharp river sand.

# STEAM-IE · No. 07 Science

#### Find the exact moisture zone for peak mechanical compaction.

![AI Image Prompt: Linear diagram showing soil states from Solid to Semi-Solid to Plastic to Liquid, highlighting Plastic Limit and Liquid Limit thresholds. Minimal vector design.]

The Plastic Limit is the exact moisture content where soil changes from a brittle solid to a moldable plastic state.

ICSEB production requires soil at its Optimum Moisture Content (OMC)—just below the Plastic Limit. 

If soil is too dry, internal friction prevents full compaction. If soil is too wet, water traps pressure and causes block deformation upon release.

# STEAM-IE · No. 08 Science
## Efflorescence Salt Attack
#### Prevent white salt crusting from destroying block surfaces over time.

![AI Image Prompt: Illustration showing water drawing soluble salts outward through porous block wall to form a white crust on the outer surface. Clean technical vector.]

Efflorescence occurs when water-soluble salts inside the soil or water migrate to the block surface as moisture evaporates.

As salt crystals form inside surface pores, they expand and cause the outer skin of the block to flake away (spalling).

Avoid using saline groundwater or unwashed beach sand. If salt is present, add pozzolanic ash to bind free salts inside the matrix.

[ACTION CALLOUT: MEASURE IT] Test your mixing water by evaporating 1 liter in a clean pan and checking for visible white salt residue.
